### ABSTRACT

Loneliness is associated with many physical and mental health problems, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Studies on trait loneliness have found evidence supporting the hypothesis that loneliness is associated with hypervigilance for social threats. However, few studies have examined this hypothesis with state loneliness. This study aims at testing the hypervigilance hypothesis of loneliness by examining the temporal dynamics between state loneliness and the momentary affect in daily life. We measured state loneliness and momentary positive and negative affect in 211 college students over a period of 13 days, using the experience sampling method. The network analysis revealed that state loneliness has a strong autoregressive effect and is positively correlated to sadness, fear, alertness, and hostility in the temporal and contemporaneous networks. In the temporal network, state loneliness formed mutually reinforced feedback loops with the feelings of alertness and hostility. The between-subject network suggested that the feeling of fear mediated the connections between loneliness and alertness, hostility, and anxiety. The centrality analysis showed that loneliness has a high strength centrality in the temporal and contemporaneous networks. These findings demonstrate how state loneliness interacted with momentary affect in daily life, indicating its association with emotional hypervigilance.

## 1. Introduction

Loneliness is an aversive experience resulting from a perceived discrepancy between people’s expected and actual social relationships, either quantitatively or qualitatively (Perlman & Peplau, 1982). It has recently been considered as a public health problem because of the statistical rise in the number of people who report feeling lonely (Griffin, 2010; Holt-Lunstad, 2017). Increased loneliness is associated with increased depressive symptoms (Cacioppo, Hawkley, & Thisted, 2010), risk of cardiovascular diseases (Valtorta, Kanaan, Gilbody, Ronzi, & Hanratty, 2016), and worsened sleep quality (Hayley et al., 2017). More seriously, the impact of loneliness on early mortality is higher than obesity and comparable to smoking (Holt-Lunstad, Smith, & Layton, 2010). Thus, there is an increasing need to study the mechanism of loneliness to understand how it harms our health.

### 1.1. The hypervigilance hypothesis of loneliness

According to the evolutionary theory of loneliness (Cacioppo et al., 2006; Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018), loneliness evolved and served as an adaptive function motivating individuals to search for social connections, as being socially connected is of evolutionary advantage. However, since solitary living and within-species aggression significantly threaten survival, loneliness also intensifies the tendency of short-term self-preservation and enhances vigilance for social threats. Hypervigilance for social threats produces attentional, confirmatory, and memorial biases in processing social information (Cacioppo & Hawkley, 2009). Compared to non-lonely individuals, lonely individuals perceive social activities as more threatening, have more negative expectations of social interactions, and remember more negative social events. Thus, lonely individuals perceive and act more negatively in social interactions, undermining their chances of forming and maintaining social relationships, ultimately resulting in a vicious circle (Cacioppo & Hawkley, 2009).

Many studies have shown that loneliness is associated with hypervigilance for social threats. Experimental studies found that individuals suffering a high level of loneliness were more likely to perceive ambiguous information as threats (Qualter et al., 2013). They are more visually attentive to negative social stimuli (Bangee, Harris, Bridges, Rotenberg, & Qualter, 2014; Qualter et al., 2013). Studies on

On the other hand, observational studies investigated the affective experience of lonely people in social interactions. Studies that used experience sampling method (ESM) revealed that, compared to socially connected individuals, lonely individuals experience more negative and less positive affect in social interactions (Hawkley, Preacher, & Cacioppo, 2007). They were also more negatively affected by negative companies, suggesting that they respond more negatively to social threats (van Roekel et al., 2014; van Roekel, Ha, Scholte, Engels, & Verhagen, 2016). Although these ESM studies did not examine the objective measures of social threat, the real-life paradigms could reflect how lonely individuals respond in actual social contexts. Therefore, they were considered as important additions to experimental studies (van Roekel et al., 2016).

Most of these studies measured loneliness as a trait, reflecting an individual’s susceptibility to loneliness in general. However, the momentary feeling of loneliness may fluctuate in daily life, influenced by different social contexts and company (van Roekel et al., 2018; Van Roekel, Scholte, Engels, Goossens, & Verhagen, 2015). Previous ESM studies examined the positive and negative affect as averaged scores. However, the relationship between loneliness and different emotions may be different. According to the hypervigilance hypothesis, as loneliness increases the tendency to perceive and act more negatively, feedback loops emerge in the relationship between state loneliness and negative momentary affect, indicative of hypervigilance. More specifically, state loneliness may increase feelings like fear, alertness, or hostility. These feelings also conversely reinforce the feeling of loneliness. Few studies have examined the temporal dynamics between state loneliness and momentary affect. The mutually reinforced loops, therefore, have not been confirmed in the studies on state loneliness.

### 1.2. The network approach

This study aims at testing the hypervigilance hypothesis of loneliness by examining the affective consequence of state loneliness. We adopted an ESM design for data collection and applied the network approach to analyze the temporal dynamics between state loneliness and momentary affect in daily life.

The ESM design is suitable for capturing the affective fluctuation, as the momentary data are typically collected several times a day for several days. Compared to traditional methods, it can minimize recall bias and ensures a higher ecological validity (Myin-Germeys et al., 2009). As the ESM data contain multiple repeated measurements nested within the participants, a multi-level modeling method can be used to disaggregate the between-subject and within-subject effects (Hamaker & Grasman, 2015).

Recently proposed network perspective has the potential to provide a more comprehensive view of dynamic affective mechanisms (Wichers, Wigman, & Myin-Germeys, 2015). From the network perspective, psychological constructs and processes can be represented as complex networks of interacting components (Borsboom, 2017; Borsboom & Cramer, 2013). For example, daily affect can be represented as a dynamic network that momentary emotions interplay with each other over time. The moment-to-moment affect dynamics may play an important role in the maintenance of psychological well-being, and specific patterns of dynamics may provide information to the emotional dysregulation (Kuppens & Verduyn, 2017).

Regarding the analysis of ESM data, recently developed network models can produce three networks that characterize different relationships between psychological variables (Epskamp, Waldorp, Möttus, & Borsboom, 2018): temporal network, contemporaneous network, and between-subject network. The temporal network is a directed network describing whether variables predict one another over time. The connections in this network can imply Granger-causality (Schuurman, Ferrer, de Boer-Sonnenschein, & Hamaker, 2016). The contemporaneous network represents within time-point correlations between variables after statistically adjusting for temporal effects. The between-subject network depicts relations between within-subject means of the variables. After the network estimation, the relative importance of the nodes in each network can be evaluated using centrality metrics (Costantini et al., 2015).

In this study, we first estimated temporal, contemporaneous, and between-subject networks from the ESM data. Thereafter, we examined the centrality index of the nodes to evaluate their relative importance. We hypothesized that, in the temporal network, loneliness could positively predict negative emotions, especially for feelings of alertness, hostility, fear, and sadness. The first three could also predict an increase in loneliness. In the contemporaneous and between-subject networks, we anticipated that loneliness would have positive correlations with negative affect and negative correlations with positive affect. As for the results of node centrality, we did not have clear expectations because few studies had used network metrics to investigate momentary affect.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Participants

The participants were recruited through social media (WeChat and QQ) and posters from among local university students under the age of 28 years. For balancing the gender ratio, only male subjects were recruited in the later stages of recruitment. A total of 232 subjects (106 males and 126 females) were included in the ESM experiment. In the statistical analysis, 21 participants were excluded due to inadequate number of responses (less than 20). The mean age of the remaining 211 participants was 19.81 years (SD = 1.36, range: 18–26), and 55.45% of them were females. The Human Participants Review Board of the university approved this study, and all participants provided written informed consent.

### 2.2. Procedure

#### 2.2.1. ESM procedure

All the recruited participants join a WeChat (A social media app in China) group, and we administered the ESM questionnaires by sending web links to this WeChat group. Over 13 days, participants were beeped to fill in the questionnaire 5 times a day (from 10 AM to 10 PM, the average time interval was about 2.5 h). They were asked to rate their then-current feelings about loneliness and other positive and negative affect on a continuous slider scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). The participants were informed that the questionnaire would be closed 30 min after the beep signal and so they needed to respond soon after receiving the beep signal.

#### 2.2.2. Measurements

We measured 10 items to examine the level of state loneliness and momentary affect. Three of them measured the level of loneliness: “isolated,” “lack companionship,” and “left out.” These items are adapted from the revised UCLA loneliness scale (Hughes, Waite, Hawkley, & Cacioppo, 2004). The average response of these three items was used to represent the level of state loneliness, denoted as “lonely.” The remaining items included three positive affect: “happy,” “relaxed,” “alert,” and four negative affect: “sad,” “afraid,” “hostile,” and “anxious.” “Afraid,” “hostile,” and “alert” were selected to indicate the emotional state of hypervigilance. ## 2.3. Statistical analysis

### 2.3.1. Descriptive statistics

The average number of responses was 54.53 (SD = 13.66), ranging from 39 to 65. For each ESM variable, a within-subject mean was calculated. Subsequently, we calculated the mean and standard deviations of these within-subject means for all variables. We also reported descriptive statistics stratified by gender.

### 2.3.2. Network estimation

The mlVAR package (version 0.4.4) in R (version 3.6.1) was used to estimate the network structures in a two-step implementation (Bringmann et al., 2013; Epskamp, Waldorp, et al., 2018). In step 1, the temporal and between-subject networks were estimated with multi-level vector autoregressive (VAR) models. Each variable was modeled by a two-level VAR model as the assessments (Level 1) were nested within subjects (Level 2). For each variable, we obtained the regression coefficients of the lag-1 predictors and partial correlation coefficients of subject-specific means. The fixed effects of lag-1 coefficients were visualized as directed connections in the temporal network. Coefficients of the subject-specific means were used to construct the between-subject network. In step 2, the contemporaneous network was estimated with a similar multi-level model from the residuals of step 1. It modeled the partial correlations between variables within the same time-points.

We estimated correlated random temporal and contemporaneous effects. The first measurement of a day was not regressed on the last measurement of the previous day. The statistical threshold was set at $\alpha = 0.05$ , and only statistically significant edges were retained. The contemporaneous and between-subject networks contain two $p$ values for each edge (i.e., one from the regression of node A to node B, the other from node B to node A). In these networks, edges were considered significant only when their $p$ values were both smaller than the threshold. We used the “Fruchterman-Reingold” algorithm to visualize the temporal network (Fruchterman & Reingold, 1991). To facilitate comparison between networks, the layouts of other two networks were set as the same as the temporal network.

### 2.3.3. Centrality analysis

We only analyzed the strength centrality of the variables as other centrality metrics are considered unsuitable for psychological networks (Bringmann et al., 2019). The strength centrality is calculated by taking the sum of all absolute weights of the directly connected edges of a node. In the directed temporal network, it is further subdivided into out-strength centrality and in-strength centrality. The former indicates how many outgoing edges a node sends to other directly connected nodes. The latter indicates how many incoming edges it receives.

### 2.3.4. Evaluating gender and age effects

Gender and age may influence the level of loneliness (Maes, Qualter, Vanhalst, Van den Noortgate, & Goossens, 2019; Mund, Freuding, Möbius, Horn, & Neyer, 2020). As the mlVAR package did not implement the option of adding subject-level covariates, we performed the following analysis to evaluate the potential gender and age effects: (1) We removed the data of 9 participants aged above 22 years and re-estimated the networks. (2) For each variable, we ran a multilevel linear regression model only including age and gender as subject-level covariates and re-estimated the networks using the residuals. We then

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Descriptive statistics

Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of within-subject means of the variables. The descriptive statistics stratified by gender are reported in the supplementary materials (Table S4).

### 3.2. The structure of networks

The estimated networks are shown in Figs. 1-3, the coefficients and $p$ values of all edges are provided in the supplementary materials (Table S1-S3).

Fig. 1 shows the estimated temporal network of the eight variables. All the remained edges are positive in sign, which means when an emotion increased it tended to increase itself and other emotions in the next time-point. The strongest temporal effects were autoregressive effects: “lonely (0.19),” “happy (0.16),” and “relaxed (0.12).” State loneliness positively predicted “sad (0.11),” “hostile (0.07),” “alert (0.06),” and “afraid (0.06).” On the other hand, “hostile (0.03),” “alert (0.05),” and “anxious (0.02)” also positively predicted loneliness. These created feedback loops between loneliness and “alert” and between loneliness and “hostile.” Although “afraid” did not positively connect to loneliness, it interconnected with the nodes predictive of loneliness: “alert (0.07),” “hostile (0.05),” and “anxious (0.07).”

Fig. 2 shows the contemporaneous network of the eight variables. The nodes can be divided into two clusters: a positive cluster containing “happy” and “relaxed,” and a negative cluster including “lonely,” “alert,” “sad,” “afraid,” “hostile,” and “anxious.” These nodes were positively and densely interconnected within the cluster and negatively connected to nodes in the other cluster. State loneliness positively connected with all the negative nodes and negatively correlated to “happy.” Among these connections, the strongest edges were “lonely” to “sad (0.18),” “lonely” to “hostile (0.17),” and “lonely” to “afraid (0.15).”

As shown in Fig. 3, the structures of between-subject and contemporaneous networks were similar. However, the connections of the between-subject network were more sparse. In this network, “lonely” positively connected to “sad,” “afraid,” and “relaxed.” It negatively connected to “happy.” It did not directly connect to “hostile” and “alert.” “Afraid” was strongly connected to “alert (0.43)” and “hostile (0.39).”

### 3.4. The effects of gender and age

The network structures remained nearly unchanged after excluding older participants. All the Pearson correlation coefficients between edge weights of the re-analyzed networks and edge weights of the original networks were above 0.99 (temporal: 0.991, contemporaneous: 0.999, between-subject: 0.998).

The network structures of the residuals were also highly consistent with the original results (all the Pearson correlation coefficients of the 3 networks were above 0.99).

## 4. Discussion

In this study, we examined the interactions between state loneliness and the other momentary affect using a network approach. State loneliness had a strong autoregressive effect and was positively correlated with daily feelings of sadness, fear, alertness, and hostility in the temporal and contemporaneous networks. In the temporal network, state loneliness formed mutually reinforced feedback loops with the feelings of alertness and hostility. These findings demonstrated how state loneliness interacted with momentary affect in daily life, indicating its association with emotional hypervigilance.

Our network analysis suggested that state loneliness is associated with emotional hypervigilance in daily life. The directed cross-lagged effects in the temporal network indicated that change of state loneliness is predictive of the negative affect, suggesting potential causal pathways. The contemporaneous connections implied that the interactions between these emotions unfold rapidly, within the time frame of two to three hours in our case. According to the evolutionary theory of loneliness, loneliness triggers hypervigilance for social threats (Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018). Consistently, previous studies found that lonely individuals allocate more attention resources to negative social stimuli (Bangee et al., 2014; Cacioppo et al., 2009; S. Cacioppo et al., 2016; Qualter et al., 2013) and perceive more negatively in social interactions (Hawkley et al., 2007; Qualter et al., 2013). In addition to the findings of trait loneliness, our study showed that state loneliness in daily life positively predicts feelings of sadness, fear, hostility, and alertness, which provided a more detailed description of affective interactions in line with the hypervigilance hypothesis. Further, the feedback loops illustrate possible pathways to the emergence of chronic loneliness.

The strong autoregressive effect in the temporal network suggested that loneliness is slow in emotional dynamics and tends to sustain over time. The autoregressive parameter is considered as a measure of emotional inertia or regulatory weakness (Hamaker & Grasman, 2015). The high emotional inertia may be one of the reasons for the association between loneliness and low psychological well-being. A recent meta-analysis investigating dynamic fluctuations in daily emotions showed that slower recovery to a normative state is associated with lower levels of psychological well-being, particularly for negative affect (Myin-Germeys et al., 2018). Consistent with our findings, a previous ESM study also found that loneliness has a strong autoregressive effect (van Winkel et al., 2017).

Altogether, these results suggested that the susceptibility of feeling afraid might play as a mediator that contributes to individual differences of loneliness.

## 4.1. Limitations

This study has several limitations. The first one relates to sample and data. The participants were not screened for neurological or psychiatric disorders. This may affect the generalizability of our conclusions as we cannot rule out the potential confounding effects of mental diseases. It is unlikely that the full emotional process was captured given the limited number of variables used in this study. Further, since we did not measure social interactions and contexts, we cannot examine how these influenced state loneliness and affective dynamics. Future studies could benefit from collecting more relevant information about daily affect, social contexts, and social interactions.

The second limitation relates to statistical methods. The stationary assumption of variables for the multi-level VAR modeling might not hold. Using a time-varying model can be helpful for future studies if more observations are available (Bringmann, Ferrer, Hamaker, Borsboom, & Tuerlinckx, 2018). We cannot evaluate the accuracy and stability of our network models as the currently available methods only apply to cross-sectional data (Epskamp, Borsboom, & Fried, 2018).

The third one relates to causal interpretation. The connections estimated in networks were analyzed based on observational data. Without experimental design, this study can only indicate potential causal relations.

## 4.2. Conclusion

Using the network approach, this study provided a more detailed description of temporal dynamics between state loneliness and momentary affect in daily life. We found that increased state loneliness is associated with emotional hypervigilance.
