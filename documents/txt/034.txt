ARTICLE INFO ABSTRACT

Keywords:

Social anxiety disorder

Cognitive therapy

Interpersonal therapy

Multilevel vector autoregressive model
Within-person networks

Analysis of longitudinal within-person networks over the course of therapy allows an identification of possible
targets of treatment. This study examined within-person networks of clinical features in social anxiety disorder
(SAD) patients during cognitive (CT) and interpersonal (IPT) therapy. Patients (n = 80) were randomized to
either CT or IPT in a 10 week residential program. They completed a measure of clinical features two times a
week. The 60 (75 %) patients who had completed at least 18 measurements were included in the analyses. The
multilevel vector autoregressive (mlVAR) model was used to analyze the data, producing a temporal, contem-
poraneous, and between-person network. In the temporal network and as expected, more homework compliance
in a half-week predicted less social anxiety and less social avoidance the subsequent half-week. Also better social
function predicted less social avoidance, whereas more social anxiety predicted more self-focus. Unexpectedly,
less social avoidance predicted more self-focus and less self-focus predicted less social function and social joy. In
the contemporaneous network, self-focus, anxiety and avoidance displayed a conditional independence struc-
ture. The estimated temporal network suggests that homework compliance and social function are promising

targets of treatment.

1. Introduction

Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is acommon disorder, epidemiological
studies across cultures indicate a lifetime prevalence of about 13 %
(Kessler et al., 2005), and is associated with severe functional impair-
ment, isolation, and the development of other clinical disorders (Fehm,
Beesdo, Jacobi, & Fiedler, 2008). A recent meta-analysis suggests that
effective psychological treatments include cognitive-behavioral therapy
(CBT), exposure, social skills training, psychodynamic therapy and
interpersonal therapy (IPT) (Mayo-Wilson et al., 2014). The results from
this meta-analysis further suggest that individual CBT has large relative
effect sizes compared to waitlist and seems to have greater effect than
most other therapies. Still, 25-50 % do not respond well to CBT or
remain considerably symptomatic at the end of treatment (Hofmann,
2007). A progress toward optimizing CBT and other effective treatments
for SAD would be to examine how they work. Identification of central
mechanisms of change could provide a basis for more focused, reliable
and effective interventions (Kazdin, 2009).

Older mechanistic studies of SAD treatments have addressed the
global level of disorder. That is, SAD has been treated as an underlying

(latent) entity causing the overt symptoms. Consequently, SAD symp-
toms - such as fear of acting in embarrassing way, anxiety in and
avoidance of social situations - are viewed as effects of SAD. Thus, their
total score is taken to indicate the severity of SAD. Correspondingly, also
proposed mechanism variables are treated as latent constructs, being
measured by total scores of observable indicators. A study of the rela-
tionship between the cognitive construct social cost — the perceived
awfulness of negative social events — and SAD symptoms is one example
(Foa, Perry, & Herbert, 1996). Thus, it is assumed that the site of causal
action resides on the global level of latent entities. Moreover, the
symptoms’ status as indicating effects of latent entities imply that they
are interchangeable and that they do not causally interact. anxiety These
assumptions are problematic, however. They are challenged by more
recent mechanistic studies of SAD treatment, in which the focus has
shifted from the level of latent disorder to the level of symptoms. These
studies have evidenced specific temporal relationships among symp-
toms, suggesting that they are causally related and have different causal
roles. For instance, both Aderka, Huppert, Davidson, and Foa (2013) and
Santoft et al. (2019) found a unidirectional effect of reduced avoidance
on subsequent decrease in social anxiety. Such findings suggest that that

focus should shift from latent disorders to individual symptoms and from

Another limitation of a lot of mechanism research is that it does not
focus on the most appropriate level of analysis. As for other disorders,
many studies of SAD treatment have focused on between-person re-
lationships, that is, the extent to which people who change less than
average on a potential mediator variable (e.g., cognitive biases) among
SAD patients also change less than average on an outcome variable (e.g.,
social anxiety). Thus, in their analysis of mechanisms, Foa, Franklin,
Perry, and Herbert (1996) examined the extent to which those patients
who showed greater decrease in social cost differed in SAD change from
those who showed less decrease. So, it is the differences between pa-
tients in their average or usual levels that are the focus of the analysis.
However, therapists are not primarily interested in how a patient differs
in his or her usual level from other patients. Rather, therapists look for
and try to facilitate deviations from the patient’s own usual level on
variables that are purported to maintain the patient’s symptoms and
therefore operate as mechanisms of change. In addition, they tend to
monitor whether such within-patient deviations in a mechanistic vari-
able (e.g., catastrophic cognitions) are followed by within-person de-
viations from the usual level of symptoms (e.g., panic attacks). Finally,
therapists check whether these deviations — if they are repeated — are
followed by improvement in the usual level of the symptoms. Thus,
therapists focus on within-patient relations between deviations from the
usual on mechanism and outcome variables to promote within-patient
changes in the usual level on outcome variables. Therefore, therapy
theories usually specify such within-person relationships between de-
viations from the usual. For instance, cognitive models of SAD (e.g.,
Clark & Wells, 1995) propose that a person will feel less socially anxious
at times when he/she appraises negative social events as less costly than
when he/she has higher cost appraisals. Thus, Foa et al.’s (1996) use of
between-person data to examine theories that focus on within-person
processes does not represent a direct test of such theories (Curran &
Bauer, 2011). Methodologists have recently pointed out that
between-person and within-person relations are different conceptually
and empirically (Molenaar, 2004) and confounding them will usually
lead to faulty conclusions (Curran & Bauer, 2011).

A study of within-person processes necessitates a collection of lon-
gitudinal data, in which potential causes (e.g., mechanisms) and
outcome are repeatedly measured over time for each individual. Because
the raw scores on the scales of time-varying variables represent a
mixture of between-person and within-person variation, these need to be
disaggregated into the two variance components. A few studies have
investigated the within-person dynamics in the treatment of SAD and
also disaggregated the studied time-varying predictors. Mortberg, Hof-
fart, Boecking, and Clark (2015) found that within-person change during
cognitive therapy (CT; Clark & Wells, 1995) from self-focused to
externally focused attention mediated improvements in SAD symptoms
one week later. Analysis of reversed mediation indicated that symptoms
did not predict later self-focus. Within-person change in SAD related
negative automatic thoughts did not predict subsequent symptoms. In a
study by our group (Hoffart, Borge, & Clark, 2016), SAD patients
received residential CT or IPT (Lipsitz, Markowitz, & Cherry, 1997).
Weekly within-person changes in process variables derived from the
cognitive model (self-focus, estimated probability and cost of negative
social events, safety behaviors) predicted subsequent changes in SAD
symptoms. Conversely, within-person changes in SAD symptoms pre-
dicted subsequent changes in self-focus, estimated probability and cost
of negative social events, but not in safety behaviors. Santoft et al.
(2019) replicated as well as improved the study of Hoffart et al. by
including separate measures of avoidance and anxiety instead of
aggregating them to a global disorder measure, and, as mentioned
above, found a unidirectional within-person effect of avoidance on
anxiety. Finally, Davis et al. (2016) examined attention bias dynamics
on a within-person level during and following CBT, using an experi-
mental spatial cueing paradigm to measure bias. They found no evidence

for attentional dysregulation as a causal mechanism for symptom
reduction in CBT for SAD. Thus, self-focus is the potential mechanism
variable most studied on a within-person level, but the data so far are
highly inconclusive. In most of the presented within-person studies, a
global level of latent entities was addressed, which is associated with the
problematic assumptions discussed above.

The shift of focus proposed above from latent disorders and mech-
anisms to individual symptoms and observable mechanisms is achieved
in the network perspective on psychopathology. In this view, concepts of
clinical conditions like SAD are taken to refer to a causal network of
observable mental problems or symptoms (observables) (Borsboom,
Cramer, Schmittmann, Epskamp, & Waldorp, 2011; Hayes, Yasinski,
Barnes, & Bockting, 2015; Hofmann, 2014). The observable symptoms
that traditionally have been considered indicators of underlying disor-
ders and states are taken to be directly related causal entities. In SAD, for
instance, the fear of showing anxiety symptoms may strengthen anxiety
as well as the urge to avoid. Moreover, increase of anxiety may
strengthen the fear of showing symptoms. Focus is moved from the level
of disorder and latent entities to the level of problems or symptoms and
their dynamics. Networks results in the enduring character of mental
problems or symptoms because transient factors in the external field (e.
g., strangers) repeatedly activate one or more symptoms which in turn
activate each other if they are densely connected (Borsboom, 2017).
Thus, disorders results from the interaction of symptoms rather than
being their cause. Symptoms are multiple because they elicit each other
and cluster in syndromes because some are usually more strongly con-
nected than others.

Symptoms are per definition the features of patients’ daily fluctu-
ating reactions that are experienced as most harmful, that is, distressing
and/or impairing (Wakefield, 1992). An urgent issue is what variables to
add to symptoms in the study of psychopathology networks (Fried &
Cramer, 2017). A reasonable extension could be counterparts of symp-
toms, for instance positive emotions (Hayes et al., 2015). Low levels of
enjoyment and positive affect (anhedonia) have been observed in SAD
(Kashdan, Weeks, & Savostyanova, 2011). Moreover, studies have evi-
denced a negative longitudinal relationship between positive affect and
subsequent social anxiety (Khazanov & Ruscio, 2016). Consistent with a
hypothesis of a maintaining role of anhedonia in SAD, Craske et al.
(2019) found that treatment focusing on increasing positive affect had
better outcomes than treatment focusing on decreasing negative affect.
The treated patients had clinically severe symptoms of depression or
anxiety, about half of them met diagnostic criteria for SAD. Thus, social
joy and social function could both influence and be influenced by social
anxiety and social avoidance. Suggestions of other relevant variables are
provided by clinical models of specific disorders (Jones, Heeren, &
McNally, 2017). For instance, the cognitive model of SAD (Clark &
Wells, 1995) holds that self-focused attention is an important main-
taining variable. Thus, according to the network view, SAD and other
disorders are maintained by the causal connections between external
triggers, symptoms, counterparts of symptoms and other activities and
processes. Consequently, it is reasonable to suppose that the mecha-
nisms of therapeutic change are to be found among these features and
connections. The effects of deactivating central features or blocking
strong connections could propagate through the network and result in
deactivation of symptoms. When in treatment, certain interventions and
activities (e.g., doing homework; Kazantzis, Whittington, & Dattilio,
2010) are purported to bring about this deactivation and blocking and
thus serve as active ingredients in treatment. Thus, treatment variables
such as homework compliance (Kazantzis et al., 2010) are potential
additional causes in the disorder network. The placement of mechanisms
within the observable elements of the disorders contrasts with the latent
variable approaches, in which mechanistic variables are seen as latent
and external to the disorders.

Network studies of SAD have so far examined cross-sectional net-
works, which are created from between-person data. Heeren and
McNally (2016) included various components of attentional control and

attentional bias measured by reaction time in laboratory tasks. They
found that the orienting component of attentional control — the selective
engagement and disengagement with certain stimuli rather than others -
was one of the central variables of the SAD network, whereas the other
components — alertness and executive control - played more peripheral
roles. Heeren and McNally (2018) found that the cross-sectional
network of fear and avoidance of social evaluative situations was
more strongly interconnected among individuals with SAD than among
individuals without SAD. Rodebaugh et al. (2018) estimated a
cross-sectional network of symptoms in a sample of SAD patients to
determine which symptoms were more central. The obtained centrality
indices successfully predicted how strongly changes in symptom items
correlated with change in the remainder of symptom items in another
SAD sample receiving CBT. However, findings were limited to the
measure used in the network and did not generalize to other measures of
SAD. The authors recommend that longitudinal studies of within-person
networks should be conducted to better discover features that are crucial
for intervention.

The purpose of this study was thus to examine the within-person
networks of clinical features in SAD patients during residential CT and
IPT. The patients were randomly assigned to the two treatments and the
outcome has been reported elsewhere, showing few differences between
the treatments at post-treatment and one-year follow-up (Borge et al.,
2008). At posttreatment, 45 (56 %) of the 80 included patients had
improved. This number increased to 56 (70 %) at one-year follow-up.
From the trial, also the mentioned process-outcome study targeting
latent constructs and based on weekly data has been published (Hoffart
et al., 2016). The selection of clinical features to be studied was based on
the considerations above. We expected that homework compliance and
self-focus would predict social anxiety and social avoidance.

2. Method
2.1. Participants

The participants were selected among referrals to specialized inpa-
tient treatment programs for SAD at a national Norwegian clinic
established for the residential treatment of non-psychotic patients that
lack adequate local treatment opportunities or have not responded
adequately to outpatient care. The inclusion criteria for the study were:
(a) satisfying DSM-IV (American Psychiatric Association, 1994) criteria
for social phobia, (b) the patient and assessor considered social phobia
the primary current diagnosis and had no immediate need for additional
treatment, (c) age 18-65 years, (d) willingness to withdraw all psy-
chotropic medication, and (e) not previously treated with similar CT or
IPT models. Patients who met these criteria were fully informed about
the study and gave written consent. The study was conducted in
compliance with the regional ethical committee. All medication was
gradually withdrawn before or within the first week of treatment. The
patients were randomly allocated to one of the two treatment condi-
tions. Eighty patients —- 41 females and 39 males with a mean age of 37.5
years (SD = 11.4) - were included and admitted to the two programs in
groups of eight, constituting five blocks of patients. The patients in the
two conditions did not differ, except that the diagnoses of panic disorder
with agoraphobia and body dysmorphic disorder were more frequent
among the CT patients (10 vs. 4 and 9 vs. 4, respectively). More details
about the sample are available elsewhere (Borge et al., 2008).

2.2. Treatments

The treatments were based on manuals for individual social phobia
treatment by cognitive therapy (Clark, 1997) or interpersonal psycho-
therapy (Lipsitz, Markowitz, & Cherry, 1997) but modified for use in a
residential setting (see Section S1 in online supplemental material). In
both CT and IPT patients received 360 min of group treatment (four
sessions) and 45-50 min (one session) of individual treatment per week.

Journal of Anxiety Disorders 76 (2020) 102312

Patients participated in the wards’ general program, consisting pri-
marily of physical exercise sessions and ward meetings. On weekends,
patients most often returned to home. In the middle of the ten-week
program, the patient groups were given one-week leave at home to
test out their new abilities in their natural environment. Further details
about the treatments are available in Section S1 in supplemental
material.

2.3. Therapists and therapist training

Two clinical psychologists, both experienced in performing cognitive
therapy for anxiety disorders, were the individual therapists in CT. A
clinical social worker with extensive psychiatric training and a psychi-
atrist were the individual therapists for the majority of IPT patients.
Both programs included three psychiatric nurses/occupational thera-
pists, who conducted the group sessions together with the individual
therapists. Details about training and supervision of the therapists are
provided elsewhere (Borge et al., 2008).

2.4. Diagnostic interviews

The SCID-I and -II interviews for assessing DSM-IV Axis I and II
disorders (First, Spitzer, Gibbon, Williams, & Benjamin, 1994; First,
Spitzer, Gibbon, Williams, & Benjamin, 1994) were conducted at pre-
treatment by the individual therapists. At one year follow-up, the Axis I
interviews were again conducted by the therapists. The interrater reli-
ability was satisfactory (Borge et al., 2008).

2.5. Integrity ratings

Competence in individual therapy sessions was rated by experts as
moderate for both treatments (Borge et al., 2008). Two graduate psy-
chology students independently rated 12 CT and 11 IPT randomly
selected group sessions with satisfactory interrater reliability (Hoffart
et al., 2016). Adherence to the treatment protocol in these group ses-
sions was rated by the students as moderate in both treatments, whereas
differentiability was rated as excellent.

2.6. Process measure

Based on the considerations in the introduction, our process measure
included two symptom variables (social avoidance, social anxiety), the
most studied mechanism variable (self-focus in social situations), two
symptom counterparts (social joy, social function), and a dimension of a
therapy-related activity: homework compliance — defined as the amount
of the agreed-upon between-session tasks that actually has been
completed. These variables were measured two times a week. Table 1
lists the process items. In case an item was taken from a standard scale,
the name of the scale is reported. Four items — covering severity of social
anxiety and social avoidance and degree of self-focus (two items) - were
taken from the Social Phobia Weekly Summary Scale (SPWSS; Clark
et al., 2003). Because one of the two self-focus items is a more general
version of the other, they were averaged to one self-focus variable.

2.7. Procedure

The process measure was completed every Thursday to obtain sum-
mary ratings of the process experiences during the most treatment
intensive part of the week and every Monday to obtain a summary rating
of the process experiences during a less treatment intensive period.
Subjects were asked to base these half-weekly ratings on their experi-
ences the last three days. To control for potential expectancy bias, the
patients were informed that the therapists were blind to the patients’
ratings.

2.8. Statistical analyses

For the main statistical method used in this study — the two-step
multilevel vector autoregressive (mIVAR) model — a number of at least
20 measurements per subject is recommended (Epskamp, Deserno, &
Bringmann, 2019). To approach this number and at the same time retain
many patients, the 11 patients who dropped out of treatment and
another 9 patients with less than 18 completed process measures were
excluded. Thus, 60 patients (75 % of the original sample of 80 patients) —
28 CT and 32 IPT patients - provided at least 18 ratings with a mean of
19.6 (range = 18-21) and were included in the analyses.

First, linear mixed models (Fitzmaurice, Laird, & Ware, 2004) were
used to examine whether the SAD features changed over the course of
treatment, and whether they changed differently in the two treatments.
For each of the dependent variables, the combination of random effects
and covariance structure of residuals that gave the best fit for the
“empty” model (the model without fixed predictors except the intercept)
was chosen. Maximum likelihood was used as estimation method.
Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) was used to compare the fit of
different models. Models that gave a reduction in AIC greater than 2 was
considered better (Burnham & Anderson, 2004). The program SPSS 25.0
was used (IBM Corp. Released, 2018).

Then, the mIVAR (Version 0.3.2) package (Epskamp et al., 2019) for
R (Version 3.5.3) (R Core Team, 2019) was used to analyze the multi-
variate time series in the 60 subjects (see Section S2 for detailed
explanation of the mIlVAR and Section S3 for the R code in supplemental
material). Three networks are estimated in mlVAR. The temporal network
represents the average lagged within-person relationships between the
variables from one half-week to the next, controlled for each other. The
contemporaneous network represents the within-person relationships be-
tween the variables within the same half-week, controlled for each other
and the temporal effects. The between-person network represents the re-
lationships between the person-means on the variables, given the
person-means on the other variables.

The mlVAR presupposes that the time series for each subject are
stationary, that is, that their means and variances-covariances are stable
over time. However, during treatment, the aim is to reduce symptoms,
which also happen in most patients. To make the time series trend sta-
tionary, we detrended the data. That is, each person’s time series was

Journal of Anxiety Disorders 76 (2020) 102312

regressed on time and the residuals were saved for further analyses.
Then we used the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS; Kwiat-
kowski, Phillips, Schmidt, & Shin, 1992) test to examine the level sta-
tionarity of these residuals, that is, whether they had a stable
variance-covariance structure over time (see details about how the
KPSS handles missing data in Section S4 in supplemental material). The
test was conducted separately for each of the 60 patients and the 6
variables per patient using the tseries (Version 0.10-47) packages
(Trapletti, Hornik, & LeBaron, 2019) for R. The KPSS test indicated that
all the time-series were level stationary. Next, the person-mean of each
variable across assessment was computed and these means and the re-
siduals computed in the first step were summed. Thus, the resulting
score represents both a person’s mean on a variable over time and the
time-specific deviations from this mean, controlled for any
person-specific linear time trend.

In networks, the links are called edges and the variables nodes. A
series of univariate models are used to estimate the parameters. In
correlated estimation, the covariances between parameters within each
of the univariate models are estimated. This is feasible for up to about
eight nodes. For more nodes, these parameter covariances are fixed to
zero, a procedure called orthogonal estimation. Although we had only six
nodes, we used orthogonal estimation to retain comparability with a
simulation study of Epskamp (2017) (see below).

The networks were thresholded by removing all effects that were not
significant. For the between-person and the contemporaneous networks,
the analysis results in two p-values for every edge — either both can be
required to be significant (“‘and”-rule) or an edge can be included if one
of the two p-values is significant (“or’-rule). We used the default “or’-
rule.

The mlVAR is an exploratory and hypothesis-generating method and
to ensure sensitivity, a significance level of p < .05 for the individual
effects in the networks was used throughout with no correction for
multiple testing (Epskamp, Waldorp, Mottus, & Borsboom, 2018).

Comparison with the mentioned simulation study of Epskamp (2017,
p. 107-111) indicated that we had sufficient power to estimate the pa-
rameters involved in our mlVAR analyses of the temporal and contem-
poraneous network. In a simulated dataset comparable to ours, Epskamp
found a sensitivity (the ability to detect true edges) and a specificity (the
ability to remove false edges) about .90. Due to the limited number of
subjects, the sensitivity for the between-person network was low.
Further details of the comparison are available in Section S5 in sup-
plemental material.

Network characteristics were also assessed in the form of centrality
indices (Opsahl, Agneessens, & Skvoretz, 2010). These parameters
indicate how central the position of a node is in the network. In undi-
rected networks, strength centrality is calculated by taking the sum of all
significant absolute edge weights. In directed networks, out-strength
centrality indicates the sum of edge weights of significant connections
going out from a node. In-strength centrality indicates the sum of edge
weights of the significant incoming connections. The centrality indices
are standardized to z-scores, that is, they are centered and divided by
their standard deviations.

Within the process measures of the 60 included patients, there were
occasional missing completion of items, amounting to 154 (2.2 %) of the
expected number of 7038. These were handled well by the maximum
likelihood estimation (Schafer & Graham, 2002) used both in the mixed
models and the mIVAR.

4. Discussion

The main purpose of the present study was to examine the temporal
and contemporaneous network of clinical features in SAD patients
during two well-documented treatments, CT and IPT. As these networks
show within-person relationships between features, they may represent
adequate tests of clinical theories — both with respect to active treatment
ingredients and mechanisms of change - and could point to important
targets for intervention. The clinical features studied included SAD

symptoms (social anxiety and social avoidance), SAD symptom coun-
terparts (social joy and social function), a proposed mechanism variable
(self-focus), and a dimension of a therapy-related activity - homework
compliance - supposed to be an active ingredient in both treatments.

As a preliminary step, we examined whether these features changed
over the course of treatment and whether they changed differently in the
two treatments. Across treatment, social anxiety and avoidance
decreased, whereas social joy and function remained unchanged.
Consistent with a more intense focus on self-focus and within-session
and between-session behavioural tasks in CT (see Section S1 in Sup-
plemental material), self-focus decreased more in CT than in IPT, while
homework compliance increased in CT and remained unchanged in IPT.

The temporal network represents the predictive relationships be-
tween the features, controlled for each other. Temporal prediction has
been called Granger causality (Granger, 1969), which is only indicative
of causality (Epskamp et al., 2018). By indicative is meant that a tem-
poral link would be expected if a causal relationship is true. However,
the link may also arise for other reasons, for instance because a third
variable is causing the two variables involved in the link. In the temporal
network estimated here, homework compliance and self-focus had high
out-strength, meaning that these features were strongly predictive of the
other features. More compliance in a half-week predicted less social
anxiety as well as less social avoidance the subsequent half-week. That
is, when a given patient completed more of his/her agreed-upon
between-session tasks than usual for him/her, subsequent anxiety and
avoidance was less than usual for him/her. These findings support that
homework compliance is an active ingredient in both treatments.

Better social function than usual predicted less than usual social
avoidance. This finding may suggest that experiencing better function
causes less subsequent avoidance.

In our previous analysis of latent constructs (also with only about
half the number of measurements included), self-focus and SAD symp-
toms (anxiety and avoidance pooled) proved to have a reciprocal within-
person relation in both treatments (Hoffart et al., 2016). In the present
network analysis, more than usual social anxiety predicted more than
usual self-focus, whereas — unexpectedly - less social avoidance predicted
more self-focus. Moreover, less than usual self-focus predicted less than
usual social function and joy. Overall, self-focus behaved for the most

part differently from what would be expected from the cognitive model
and from that self-focus was explicitly targeted in CT. One may speculate
that the findings reflect an oscillating process, based on the social fatigue
often reported by SAD patients. More social exposure (less avoidance)
and less self-focus than usual may lead to more than usual fatigue, which
is reflected in relatively more self-focus and less function and joy the
next half-week. Consistent with this interpretation, an experience sam-
pling study of non-clinical subjects found that immediate increases in
positive affect after extraverted behavior were followed by fatigue
(Leikas & Ilmarinen, 2017).

Two of the six features — social avoidance and social function - had
negative self-loops. Such negative self-loops may reflect an oscillation
process like the one depicted above or a methodological artefact, known
as Nickell’s Bias, which may occur when the time series are relatively
short (Nickell, 1981).

The contemporaneous network represents the associations of fea-
tures within the same half-week window of measurement, controlled for
each other and the temporal effects. Whenever the patients completed
more of their agreed-upon homework than usual for them, they were
more likely to avoid less, function better in social situations, and enjoy
social contact more than usual. The “cost” was that they also were more
likely to have more social anxiety than usual in the short term. However,
the temporal network suggests that more compliance leads to less anx-
iety in the longer term (next half-week). Cooper et al. (2017) studied
homework in prolonged exposure for posttraumatic stress disorder and
found — consistent with our contemporaneous networks - that higher
adherence to imaginal exposure homework in a between-session period
was associated with greater symptom improvement in the same period.
However, the contemporaneous network is not directed and although its
connections probably reflect causal processes occurring within the
half-week, their direction is undecided. Thus, given the data, avoidance,
function and joy may influence homework compliance as well as the
other way around.

In certain circumstances, however, the connections of the contem-
poraneous network may indicate causal relationships occurring within
the window of measurement. Causality is here reflected in conditional
independence structures (Pearl, 2000). For SAD, a hypothesis derived
from the cognitive model would be that self-focus increases social
 A. Hoffart and S.U. Johnson

anxiety, which, in turn, strengthens social avoidance. This causal
structure indicates that avoidance is to be expected if self-focus is high.
However, also if we know that anxiety is high, we will expect avoidance,
and knowing self-focus will not improve this prediction. This means that
self-focus and avoidance are conditionally independent, given anxiety.
This partial correlation structure is actually exhibited in the contem-
poraneous network. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis of a
causal effect of self-focus. However, conditional independence is also
only indicative of causality, as different causal models are compatible
with the exact same conditional independence structure. For instance,
anxiety may cause both avoidance and self-focus.

The between-person network was of secondary interest in this study
and had low power given the limited number of subjects. The resulting
low sensitivity was probably reflected in the few significant connections.
However, E:pskamp’s (2017) simulation study suggests that specificity is
reasonable, so that positive findings can be trusted. Similar to the
contemporaneous network, patients who, on average and compared to
other patients, completed more of their agreed-upon homework also, on
average and compared to other patients, had less social avoidance but
more social anxiety. Also as in the contemporaneous network, self-focus
was related to social avoidance only through social anxiety.

A strength of this study was that the network focus on observables
instead of latent constructs led to more differentiated findings (e.g., the
relationships between self-focus, anxiety and avoidance discussed
above). From such findings, more specific clinical recommendations can
be inferred. Another strength was that within-person rather than
between-person relationships were examined. This is an asset because
therapy theories concern how within-person change in a mechanism
variable relates to subsequent within-person change in an outcome
variable during therapy. Such knowledge informs therapists what vari-
ables could be affected to achieve patient improvement. A third strength
was that the time lag of a half-week was shorter than the lag of a week
used in previous process studies of SAD treatments (e.g., Mortberg et al.,
2015).

Several limitations need to be mentioned. First, the number of
repeated observations per patient (M = 19.6, range = 18-21) was at the
border of the number recommended for the mlVAR analysis. Within-
person centering with less than 20 measurements per person may lead
to biased estimates, most notably to negative auto-regressive effects
(Nickell’s bias) (Epskamp et al., 2019). As far as we know, the influences
of time series about or less than 20 on cross-regressive effects are not
tested out yet. Still, comparison with Epskamp’s (2017) simulation study
of mIVAR suggested that we had sufficient power with regard to our
temporal and contemporaneous networks, which were the focus of this
study. Second, because sub-group analyses would require additional
power, we chose not to investigate network differences between treat-
ments. Third, the exploratory nature of the study with no correction for
multiple testing increases the risk for false positive findings. Fourth, the
centrality measures used are developed for social networks, and it is
unclear to what extent they reflect the relationships between psycho-
logical variables (Bringmann et al., 2019). In particular, do these mea-
sures capture the flow processes of dynamic psychological networks?
For instance, a SAD patient’s corrective experiences during completion
of a homework task would influence both anxiety and urge to avoid at
the next social occasion. Thus, it is plausible that a node affects those
that it is directly connected to in a parallel way. Such a one-lag parallel
flow can be modeled with strength centrality, which we have used in the
present study. It is more questionable, however, whether the importance
of variables is validly reflected in the unique and not shared explained
variance of a node produced by VAR models (Bringmann et al., 2019).
Fifth, although the present time lag of a half-week represent an
improvement compared to previous studies, the fast effects of SAD
processes indicate that a time scale of several measurements a day
should be used. Sixth, our sample scored higher on general symptoms
measures and unemployment and had a higher proportion of partici-
pants who had not responded to previous therapy than the samples in

other SAD trials (Borge et al., 2008, p. 1005). This may limit the
generalizability of the findings.

In conclusion, the estimated temporal network suggest that one
could increase the effect of CT and IPT on SAD through more emphasis
on homework compliance and social function. Although less persuasive
with regard to causality, the contemporaneous network is consistent
with the importance of homework compliance, social function and self-
focus as targets of intervention. Future network studies of SAD treatment
should include possible determinants (e.g., expectancies, willingness to
engage) of homework compliance (Kazantzis et al., 2010). In particular,
studies of SAD treatment should examine whether the unexpected
findings with respect to the role of self-focus is replicated, and what
mechanisms are responsible for the relationships. Future studies require
an extended list of SAD symptoms as well as mechanistic variables
derived from therapy models of SAD and shortened time lags involving
more intensive experience sampling methods should be used. Increasing
the number of measurements (preferably to at least 100; see Epskamp
et al., 2018) enables a reliable estimation of networks for each patient,
allowing a bottom-up approach to reveal common processes occurring
across individuals (Fisher, Reeves, Lawyer, Medaglia, & Rubel, 2017). A
high number of measurements also allows estimation of change of pa-
rameters for multivariate time series over periods in therapy (Haslbeck,
Bringmann, & Waldorp, 2019). This is relevant for research because an
aim of network-focused treatments would be to dilute some central
connections between clinical features, that is, to change parameters.
